''[[Sharia]]'' means Islamic law based on Islamic concepts based from Quran and Hadith. Since the early Islamic states of the eighth and ninth centuries, Sharia always existed alongside other [[normative]] systems.<ref>{{cite book|last=Otto|first=Jan Michiel|title=Sharia Incorporated: A Comparative Overview of the Legal Systems of Twelve Muslim Countries in Past and Present|year=2009|publisher=Leiden University Press|location=Leiden|isbn=978-9087280574|pages=615–16|url=http://dare.uva.nl/document/221087|access-date=13 November 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131105224404/http://dare.uva.nl/document/221087|archive-date=5 November 2013|url-status=live}}</ref>

Historically, Sharia was interpreted by independent jurists (''[[Faqīh|Faqihs]]''), based on Islamic scriptural sources and various legal methodologies.<ref name=vikor/> In the modern era, statutes inspired by European codes replaced traditional laws in most parts of the Muslim world, with classical Sharia rules retained mainly in [[Status (law)|personal status]] laws. Countries such as Pakistan and Saudi Arabia have Islam as their state religion, but haven’t implemented Sharia law fully.<ref name=vikor/><ref name="auto">{{cite book |first1=Jan Michiel |last1=Otto |year=2008 |title=Sharia and National Law in Muslim Countries: Tensions and Opportunities for Dutch and EU Foreign Policy |publisher=Amsterdam University Press |isbn=978-9087280482 |url=https://openaccess.leidenuniv.nl/bitstream/handle/1887/20694/Sharia%20and%20national%20Law%20in%20Muslim%20countries.pdf |page=19 |access-date=22 January 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171009235043/https://openaccess.leidenuniv.nl/bitstream/handle/1887/20694/Sharia%20and%20national%20Law%20in%20Muslim%20countries.pdf |archive-date=9 October 2017 |url-status=live }}</ref> These laws were codified by legislative bodies which sought to modernize them without abandoning their foundations in traditional jurisprudence.<ref name=vikor/><ref name=mayer/> The [[Islamic revival]] of the late 20th century brought along calls by [[Islamist]] movements for full implementation of Sharia, including ''[[hudud]]'' [[Capital punishment in Islam|capital punishments]], which in some cases resulted in traditionalist legal reform.<ref name=vikor/><ref name=mayer/> Some countries with Muslim minorities use Sharia-based laws to regulate [[Islamic Banking|banking]], [[islamic economics|economics]], inheritance, marriage and other governmental and personal affairs of their Muslim population. The use of Sharia in non-Muslim countries and on non-Muslims is debated.

==Historical background==
{{Main|Sharia}}

[[Sharia]] is a [[religious law]] forming part of the [[Islam]]ic tradition.<ref name=oxforddic>{{cite web |title=British & World English: sharia |url=https://www.oxforddictionaries.com/definition/english/sharia |publisher=Oxford University Press |access-date=4 December 2015 |location=Oxford |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20151208120345/https://www.oxforddictionaries.com/definition/english/sharia |archive-date=8 December 2015 |url-status=dead }}</ref> Traditional [[Principles of Islamic jurisprudence|theory of Islamic jurisprudence]] recognizes four [[sources of sharia|sources of Sharia]]: the Quran, ''[[sunnah]]'' (authentic hadith), ''[[qiyas]]'' (analogical reasoning), and ''[[ijma]]'' (juridical consensus).<ref name="Esposito, John 2001">[[John Esposito|John L. Esposito]], [[Natana J. Classical jurisprudence was elaborated by private [[Ulama|religious scholars]], largely through legal opinions ([[fatwa|''fatwas'']]) issued by qualified jurists ([[mufti|''muftis'']]). It was historically applied in [[Sharia court]]s by ruler-appointed [[Qadi|judges]], who dealt mainly with civil disputes and community affairs.<ref name=ODI/><ref name=calder/> [[Mazalim|Sultanic courts]], the [[Shurta|police]] and [[Muhtasib|market inspectors]] administered criminal justice, which was influenced by Sharia but not bound by its rules.<ref>{{cite encyclopedia |first=Farhat J. |last=Ziadeh |title=Criminal Law |encyclopedia=The Oxford Encyclopedia of the Islamic World |editor=John L. </ref> and ''fiqh'' was complemented by various economic, criminal and administrative laws issued by Muslim rulers.<ref>{{cite encyclopedia|first=Devin J.|last=Stewart|title=Shari'a|editor=Gerhard Böwering, Patricia Crone|encyclopedia=The Princeton Encyclopedia of Islamic Political Thought|publisher=Princeton University Press|year=2013|page=500}}</ref> The Ottoman [[Mecelle|civil code of 1869–1876]] was the first partial attempt to codify Sharia.<ref name=mayer>{{cite encyclopedia |first=Ann Elizabeth |last=Mayer |title=Law. In the modern era, traditional laws in the Muslim world have been widely replaced by statutes inspired by European models.<ref name=vikor/><ref name="auto"/> Judicial procedures and legal education were likewise brought in line with European practice.<ref name=vikor/> While the constitutions of most Muslim-majority states contain references to Sharia, its classical rules were largely retained only in [[Family law|personal status law]].<ref name=vikor/> Legislators who codified these laws sought to modernize them without abandoning their foundations in traditional jurisprudence.<ref name=vikor/><ref name=mayer/> The [[Islamic revival]] of the late 20th century brought along calls by [[Islamist]] movements for full implementation of Sharia, including ''[[hudud]]'' capital punishments and other forms of punishment like [[qisas]] and [[Blood money in Islam|diyah]] In some cases, this resulted in traditionalist legal reform, while other countries witnessed juridical reinterpretation of Sharia advocated by progressive reformers.<ref name=vikor/><ref name=mayer/><ref>{{cite encyclopedia |first=Intisar A. |last=Rabb |author-link=Intisar A. ==Types of legal systems==
The legal systems of Muslim countries may be grouped as: secular systems, mixed systems, and classical Sharia systems.<ref name="Otto2008">{{cite book|last=Otto|first=Jan Michiel|title=Sharia and National Law in Muslim Countries|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=f9XaP-ufhPQC&pg=PA8|access-date=11 March 2013|date=30 August 2008|publisher=Amsterdam University Press|isbn=978-9087280482|pages=8–9|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160115125020/https://books.google.com/books?id=f9XaP-ufhPQC&pg=PA8|archive-date=15 January 2016|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Secular systems===

Secular systems are those where Sharia plays no role in the nation's legal system and religious interference in state affairs, politics, and law is not permitted. [[Turkey]] has been an example of a Muslim-majority nation with a secular system.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Kuru |first=Ahmet |date=July 17, 2020 |title=Hagia Sophia, Islamism, and Secularism in Turkey |url=https://berkleycenter.georgetown.edu/responses/hagia-sophia-islamism-and-secularism-in-turkey |access-date=2023-11-20 |website=Berkley Center for Religion, Peace and World Affairs |language=en}}</ref> Several states in West Africa and Central Asia also describe themselves as secular.<ref name="Otto2008"/>

===Mixed systems===

Most Muslim countries have mixed legal systems that postulate a constitution and [[Rule of Law|rule of law]], while also allowing rules of [[Fiqh|traditional Islamic jurisprudence]] to influence certain areas of national law. These systems possess large bodies of codified laws, which may be based on European or Indian codes. In these systems, the central legislative role is played by politicians and modern jurists rather than traditional religious scholars. [[Pakistan]], [[Egypt]], [[Malaysia]], and [[Nigeria]] are examples of states having mixed systems.<ref name="Otto2008"/> Some states with Muslim minorities, such as [[Greece]] ([[Western Thrace]] region only) and [[Israel]], also have mixed systems that administer Sharia for their Muslim population.<ref name="The Sharia Courts">{{cite web|website=Israel Ministry of Justice|title=The Sharia Courts|url=https://www.justice.gov.il/En/Units/ShariaCourts/Pages/default.aspx|access-date=19 March 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190325005530/https://www.justice.gov.il/En/Units/ShariaCourts/Pages/default.aspx|archive-date=25 March 2019|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{Cite journal |last=Tsaoussi |first=Aspasia |last2=Zervogianni |first2=Eleni |date=2011-05-25 |title=Multiculturalism and Family Law: The Case of Greek Muslims |url=https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228144363_Multiculturalism_and_Family_Law_The_Case_of_Greek_Muslims |journal=Intersentia |pages=1}}</ref>

===Classical Sharia systems===

Under this system, shared by a small minority of modern countries, classical Sharia is formally equated with national law and to a great extent provides its substance.  The state has a ruler who functions as the highest judiciary and may promulgate and modify laws in some legal domains, but traditional religious scholars (''[[ulama]]'') play a decisive role in interpreting Sharia. The classical Sharia system is exemplified by [[Saudi Arabia]] and some [[Gulf states (Middle East)|Gulf states]]. [[Iran]] shares many of the same features, but also possesses characteristics of mixed legal systems, such as a parliament and codified laws.<ref name="Otto2008"/>

== Domains of application ==

===Constitutional law===

Most Muslim-majority countries incorporate Sharia at some level in their legal framework. Their constitutions commonly refer to Sharia as a source or the main source of law, though these references are not in themselves indicative of how much the legal system is influenced by Sharia, and whether the influence has a traditionalist or modernist character.<ref name=vikor/><ref name=calder/> The same constitutions usually also refer to universal principles such as democracy and human rights, leaving it up to legislators and the judiciary to work out how these norms are to be reconciled in practice.<ref name="otto-18-20"/> Conversely, some countries (e.g., [[Algeria]]), whose constitution does not mention Sharia, possess Sharia-based family laws.<ref name=calder/> Nisrine Abiad identifies [[Bahrain]], Iran, Pakistan, and Saudi Arabia as states with "strong constitutional consequences" of Sharia "on the organization and functioning of power".<ref>{{cite book|first=Nisrine|last=Abiad|title=Sharia, Muslim States and International Human Rights Treaty Obligations: A Comparative Study|publisher=British Institute of International and Comparative Law|year=2008|pages=38–42}}</ref>

===Family law===

Except for secular systems, Muslim-majority countries possess Sharia-based family laws (marriage, inheritance, etc.). These laws generally reflect influence of various modern-era reforms and tend to be characterized by ambiguity, with traditional and modernist interpretations often manifesting themselves in the same country, both in legislation and court decisions. In [[Greece]] (only in [[Western Thrace]]), [[Indonesia]] (outside of [[Aceh]], and in most circumstances), Nigeria (outside the [[States of Nigeria|states]] using Sharia law) and [[Senegal]] (only in inheritance) people can choose whether to pursue a case in a Sharia or secular court.<ref name=otto-18-20/>

===Criminal law===

Countries in the [[Muslim world]] generally have criminal codes influenced by [[Law of France|French law]] or [[common law]], and in some cases a combination of Western legal traditions. In the course of Islamization campaigns, several countries (Libya, Pakistan, Iran, Sudan, Mauritania, and Yemen) inserted Islamic criminal laws into their penal codes, which were otherwise based on Western models. In some countries only ''hudud'' penalties were added, while others also enacted provisions for ''[[qisas]]'' (law of retaliation) and ''[[Diya (Islam)|diya]]'' (monetary compensation). Iran subsequently issued a new [[Islamic Penal Code of Iran|"Islamic Penal Code"]]. Saudi Arabia has never adopted a criminal code and Saudi judges still follow traditional Hanbali jurisprudence. The criminal code of Afghanistan contain a general provision that certain crimes are to be punished according to Sharia, without specifying the penalties. In United Arab Emirates, Sharia in criminal law is only applicable in determining ''diyah'' amounts.<ref name=":73" /> Some Nigerian states have also enacted Islamic criminal laws. [[Islamic criminal law in Aceh|Laws in the Indonesian province of Aceh]] provide for application of discretionary (''[[ta'zir]]'') punishments for violation of Islamic norms, but explicitly exclude ''hudud'' and ''qisas''.<ref>{{cite encyclopedia|first=Silvia|last=Tellenbach|year=2015|title=Islamic Criminal Law|encyclopedia=The Oxford Handbook of Criminal Law|editor1=Markus D. Dubber|editor2= Tatjana Hornle|publisher= Oxford University Press|pages=249–250}} {{ISBN|978-0199673599}}</ref> [[Brunei]] has been implementing a "Sharia Penal Code", which includes provisions for stoning and [[Amputation in Islamic Law|amputation]], in stages since 2014.<ref>{{cite news|newspaper=The New York Times|author=Austin Ramzy|title=Brunei to Punish Adultery and Gay Sex With Death by Stoning|date=28 March 2019|url=https://www.nytimes.com/2019/03/28/world/asia/brunei-adultery-gay-stoning.html}}</ref><ref>{{cite news|title=Brunei Shariah law applies death sentence for homosexuality|newspaper=Deutsche Welle|date=27 March 2019|url=https://www.dw.com/en/brunei-shariah-law-applies-death-sentence-for-homosexuality/a-48076114|access-date=13 April 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190331093514/https://www.dw.com/en/brunei-shariah-law-applies-death-sentence-for-homosexuality/a-48076114|archive-date=31 March 2019|url-status=live}}</ref> The countries where ''hudud'' penalties are legal do not use stoning and amputation routinely, and generally apply other punishments instead.<ref name=vikor/><ref name=otto-18-20/><ref>{{cite web|first=Jonathan A. C.|last=Brown|title=Stoning and Hand Cutting—Understanding the Hudud and the Shariah in Islam|year=2017|access-date=24 March 2019|website=Yaqeen Institute|url=https://yaqeeninstitute.org/jonathan-brown/stoning-and-hand-cutting-understanding-the-hudud-and-the-shariah-in-islam/|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190406221119/https://yaqeeninstitute.org/jonathan-brown/stoning-and-hand-cutting-understanding-the-hudud-and-the-shariah-in-islam/|archive-date=6 April 2019|url-status=live}}</ref>

==Africa==
===Algeria===
Article 222 of the Family Code of 1983 specifies Sharia as the residuary source of laws.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/Algeria.htm |title=Algeria |publisher=Law.emory.edu |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130212041041/http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/Algeria.htm |archive-date=2013-02-12 |url-status=live }}</ref> According to the [[United States Department of State|U.S. State Department]], the Sharia-derived family code treats women as minors under the legal guardianship of a husband or male relative, though in practice the implied restrictions are not uniformly enforced.<ref>{{cite web|title=Algeria – Constitution and Laws|url=https://2009-2017.state.gov/documents/organization/171731.pdf|publisher=U.S. State Department (2011)|access-date=3 October 2014}}</ref>

===Benin===
It has a civil law system with influences from [[customary law]].<ref name=wf-legal>{{cite web|title=Legal Systems|url=https://www.cia.gov/library/publications/the-world-factbook/fields/2100.html|publisher=The World Factbook|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140518174611/https://www.cia.gov/library/publications/the-world-factbook/fields/2100.html|archive-date=2014-05-18|url-status=dead}}</ref>

===Burkina Faso===
It has a civil law system.<ref name=wf-legal/>

===Cameroon===
It has a mixed legal system of English common law, French civil law, and customary law.<ref name=wf-legal/>

===Chad===
After gaining independence from [[France]], [[Chad]] retained the French legal system.<ref>{{cite web|last1=Ngarhodjim|first1=Nadjita F.|title=An Introduction to the Legal System and Legal Research in Chad|url=http://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/Chad1.htm|website=[[GlobaLex]]|publisher=New York University School of Law|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170606135533/http://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/Chad1.htm|archive-date=2017-06-06|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Comoros===
The legal system is based on both Sharia and remnants of the French legal code.<ref>{{cite web|title=Freedom in the World – Comoros (2002)|url=http://www.unhcr.org/refworld/country,,FREEHOU,,COM,,473c53b923,0.html|publisher=UNHCR Refworld}}</ref> According to the article 229-7 of the Penal Code, any Muslim who makes use of products forbidden by Sharia can be punished by imprisonment of up to six months.<ref>{{cite web|title=Loi N°81-006, modifiée par les lois 87-004 et 95-012 portant Code pénal|url=http://www.ministerejustice.gouv.km/v1/IMG/pdf/RECEUIL_DES_TEXTES_LEGISLATIFS.pdf|publisher=Ministère de la Justice|access-date=24 February 2013|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130530070502/http://www.ministerejustice.gouv.km/v1/IMG/pdf/RECEUIL_DES_TEXTES_LEGISLATIFS.pdf|archive-date=2013-05-30|url-status=dead}}</ref>

===Côte d'Ivoire===
It has a civil law system.<ref name=wf-legal/>

===Djibouti===
The Family Code is mainly derived from Sharia and regulates personal status matters such as marriage, divorce, child custody and inheritance.<ref>{{cite web|title=Djibouti Gender Equality Profile|url=http://www.unicef.org/gender/files/Djibouti-Gender-Eqaulity-Profile-2011.pdf|publisher=UNICEF|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170620105303/https://www.unicef.org/gender/files/Djibouti-Gender-Eqaulity-Profile-2011.pdf|archive-date=2017-06-20|url-status=live}}</ref> Sharia does not apply to criminal law.<ref>{{cite web|title=24ème session|url=http://www.ohchr.org/fr/NewsEvents/Pages/DisplayNews.aspx?NewsID=3146&LangID=F|publisher=Haut-Commissariat aux droits de l'homme|access-date=9 July 2014|quote=La Charia n'est pas compétente dans le domaine pénal, a précisé la délégation.|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140714161716/http://www.ohchr.org/fr/NewsEvents/Pages/DisplayNews.aspx?NewsID=3146&LangID=F|archive-date=2014-07-14|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Egypt===
Article 2 of Egypt's 2014 Constitution declares the principles of Sharia to be the main source of legislation.<ref>{{cite web|author=Adly Mansour|title=Egypt's Constitution|url=http://www.sis.gov.eg/Newvr/Dustor-en001.pdf|publisher=Government of Egypt|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150718052913/http://www.sis.gov.eg/Newvr/Dustor-en001.pdf|archive-date=2015-07-18|url-status=live}}</ref> Egypt's law and enforcement system are in flux since its [[Egypt Revolution of 2011|2011 revolution]];<ref>{{cite web|last1=Abdel Wahab|first1=Mohamed S. E.|title=An Overview of the Egyptian Legal System and Legal Research|url=https://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/Egypt1.htm|website=GlobaLex|publisher=New York University School of Law|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171123153543/http://www.nyulawglobal.org/Globalex/Egypt1.htm|archive-date=2017-11-23|url-status=live}}</ref> however, the declaration of Sharia's primacy in Article 2 is a potential ground for unconstitutionality of any secular laws in Egyptian legal code.<ref>{{cite web|title=Egypt – The Judiciary, Civil Rights and the Rule of Law|url=http://countrystudies.us/egypt/115.htm|publisher=Library of Congress, USA|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160304223532/http://countrystudies.us/egypt/115.htm|archive-date=2016-03-04|url-status=live}}</ref> Sharia courts and judges are run and licensed by the [[Ministry of Justice (Egypt)|Ministry of Justice]].<ref>{{cite news |url=https://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/7235357.stm |title=Incorporating Sharia into legal systems |work=BBC News |date=8 February 2008 |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130425031043/http://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/7235357.stm |archive-date=2013-04-25 |url-status=live }}</ref> The personal status law that regulates matters such as marriage, divorce and child custody is governed by Sharia. In a family court, a woman's testimony is worth half of a man's testimony.<ref>{{cite web|title=Egypt Gender Equality Profile|url=http://www.unicef.org/gender/files/Egypt-Gender-Eqaulity-Profile-2011.pdf|publisher=UNICEF|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181019172446/https://www.unicef.org/gender/files/Egypt-Gender-Eqaulity-Profile-2011.pdf|archive-date=2018-10-19|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Eritrea===
Muslims are required to use Sharia courts for cases dealing with marriage, inheritance and family of Muslims.<ref>{{cite web|title=Introduction To Eritrean Legal System And Research|url=http://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/Eritrea.htm|website=GlobaLex|publisher=New York University School of Law|access-date=19 February 2013|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130318023906/http://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/Eritrea.htm|archive-date=2013-03-18|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Ethiopia===
Muslims can choose Sharia law only if all parties consent for cases regarding marriage, divorce, maintenance, guardianship of minors (only if both parties are Muslims). Also included are cases concerning ''[[waqf]]s'', gifts, succession, or wills, provided that donor is a Muslim or deceased was a Muslim at time of death. The consensual model was established because of the Kedija Bashir et al. case, where the House of Federation firmly established that sharia law can only hear cases if all parties consent therefore replacing the previously exclusive powers of sharia personal law.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/Ethiopia.htm |title=Ethiopia |publisher=Law.emory.edu |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130212024626/http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/Ethiopia.htm |archive-date=2013-02-12 |url-status=live }}</ref>

===Gabon===
It has a mixed legal system of French civil law and customary law.<ref name=wf-legal/>

===The Gambia===
Article 7 of the [[Constitution of the Gambia|constitution]] identifies Sharia as source of law in matters of personal status and inheritance among members of communities to which it applies.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/gambia.htm |title=Gambia |publisher=Law.emory.edu |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130212052612/http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/gambia.htm |archive-date=2013-02-12 |url-status=live }}</ref>

===Ghana===
Ghana is a secular state. Any other laws inconsistent with the national constitution are deemed null and void. No religious laws are applied in civil or criminal cases.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/ghana.htm |title=Ghana |publisher=Law.emory.edu |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130212041102/http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/ghana.htm |archive-date=2013-02-12 |url-status=live }}</ref>

===Guinea-Bissau===
It has a mixed legal system of civil law and customary law.<ref name=wf-legal/>

===Guinea===
It has a civil law system.<ref name="cfr-gov-sharia">{{cite web |url=http://www.cfr.org/religion/islam-governing-under-sharia/p8034 |title=Islam: Governing Under Sharia |publisher=Council on Foreign Relations |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130203022058/http://www.cfr.org/religion/islam-governing-under-sharia/p8034 |archive-date=2013-02-03 |url-status=live }}</ref>

===Kenya===
Sharia is applied by [[Kadhi courts|Kadhis courts]] where "all the parties profess the Muslim religion".<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/kenya.htm |title=Kenya |publisher=Law.emory.edu |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130116220016/http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/kenya.htm |archive-date=2013-01-16 |url-status=live }}</ref> Under article 170, section 5 of the [[Constitution of Kenya|constitution]], the jurisdiction of Kadhis' court is limited to matters relating to "personal status, marriage, divorce or inheritance in proceedings in which all the parties profess the Muslim religion, and Muslims are then required to use the jurisdiction of the Kadhi’s courts".<ref>{{cite web|title=The Constitution of Kenya|url=http://www.kenyaembassy.com/pdfs/The%20Constitution%20of%20Kenya.pdf|publisher=Embassy of the Republic of Kenya, Washington DC|access-date=24 February 2013|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121223095029/http://kenyaembassy.com/pdfs/The%20Constitution%20of%20Kenya.pdf|archive-date=23 December 2012|url-status=dead}}</ref>

===Libya===
[[Muammar Gaddafi]] merged civil and Sharia courts in 1973. Civil courts now employ Sharia judges who sit in regular courts of appeal and specialise in Sharia appellate cases.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/libya.htm |title=Libya |publisher=Law.emory.edu |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130212203803/http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/libya.htm |archive-date=2013-02-12 |url-status=live }}</ref> The personal status laws are derived from Sharia.<ref>{{cite web|title=Libya Gender Equality Profile|url=http://www.unicef.org/gender/files/Libya-Gender-Eqaulity-Profile-2011.pdf|publisher=UNICEF|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130502222708/http://www.unicef.org/gender/files/Libya-Gender-Eqaulity-Profile-2011.pdf|archive-date=2013-05-02|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Mali===
It has a civil law system influenced by customary law.<ref name=wf-legal/> In urban areas, positive law is common. In rural areas the customary law usually dominates. Local rural versions of Sharia are the predominant sources for customary law.<ref>{{cite web|author=Servaas Feiertag|year=2008|url=http://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/Mali1.htm|title=Guide to Legal Research in Mali|website=GlobaLex|publisher=New York University School of Law|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160305014333/http://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/Mali1.htm|archive-date=2016-03-05|url-status=live}}</ref> Article 25 in Title II of Mali's constitution declares it to be a secular state.<ref>Jeffrey Craver (Translator), [http://confinder.richmond.edu/admin/docs/Mali.pdf The Constitution of the Republic of Mali] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120912141413/http://confinder.richmond.edu/admin/docs/Mali.pdf |date=2012-09-12 }} University of Richmond School of Law</ref>

===Mauritania===
The Penal Code contains Sharia crimes such as [[heresy]], [[apostasy]], [[atheism]], refusal to pray, [[adultery]] and [[alcohol consumption]]. Punishments include stoning, amputation and lashing.<ref>{{cite web|title=Researching the Legal System and Laws of the Islamic Republic of Mauritania|url=http://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/mauritania.htm|website=GlobaLex|publisher=New York University School of Law|access-date=19 February 2013|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130531131932/http://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/mauritania.htm|archive-date=31 May 2013|url-status=dead}}</ref>

===Morocco===
In 1956, a Code of Personal Status (''[[Mudawana]]'') was issued, based on dominant [[Maliki]] school of Sharia jurisprudence. Regional Sharia courts also hear personal status cases on appeal.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/morocco.htm |title=Morocco |publisher=Law.emory.edu |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20081201012921/http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/morocco.htm |archive-date=2008-12-01 |url-status=live }}</ref> In matters of family law, a woman's testimony is worth only half of that of a man.<ref>{{cite web|title=Morocco Gender Equality Profile|url=http://www.unicef.org/gender/files/Morocco-Gender-Eqaulity-Profile-2011.pdf|publisher=UNICEF|access-date=11 March 2013|archive-date=24 April 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210424151431/https://www.unicef.org/gender-equality|url-status=dead}}</ref> With 2003 reforms of its criminal law, Article 222 of its new criminal code is derived from Sharia; Articles 220–221, 268–272 of its criminal law similarly codify those activities as crimes that are prohibited under Sharia.<ref>{{cite book|last1=Buskens|first1=Leon|editor1-last=Otto|editor1-first=Jan|title=Sharia Incorporated|url=https://archive.org/details/shariaincorporat00otto|url-access=limited|publisher=Leiden University Press|isbn=978-9087280574|pages=[https://archive.org/details/shariaincorporat00otto/page/n122 122]–24|year=2010}}</ref> Morocco adopted a new constitution in 2011; Article 41 of this constitution granted sole power to the Superior Council of the Ulemas to guide its laws through ''fatwas''.<ref>{{cite web|title=Constitution of Morocco 2011|url=http://www.maroc.ma/en/content/constitution|publisher=Government of Morocco, Article 41|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181110200240/http://www.maroc.ma/en/content/constitution|archive-date=2018-11-10|url-status=live|date=11 April 2013}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|title=Morocco Laws Criminalizing Apostasy|url=https://www.loc.gov/law/help/apostasy/index.php#morocco|publisher=Library of Congress, U.S. Government (2013)|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180104183829/http://www.loc.gov/law/help/apostasy/index.php#morocco|archive-date=2018-01-04|url-status=live|date=June 2014}}</ref>

===Mozambique===
Paula Rainha states that, "Mozambique’s legal system can be considered civil law based (at least the formal legal system) and legislation is the primary source of law."<ref>{{cite web|last1=Rainha|first1=Paula|title=Republic of Mozambique – Legal System and Research|url=http://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/Mozambique.htm#_2._Legal_Sources|website=GlobaLex|publisher=New York University School of Law|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180501104023/http://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/Mozambique.htm#_2._Legal_Sources|archive-date=2018-05-01|url-status=live}}</ref>

Sharia was adopted in 2009.<ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.reuters.com/article/idUSLA575453 |title=Somali cabinet votes to implement sharia law |publisher=Reuters |date=2009-03-10 |access-date=2013-02-18 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121023065759/http://www.reuters.com/article/2009/03/10/idUSLA575453 |archive-date=2012-10-23 |url-status=live }}</ref> Article 2 of Somali 2012 Constitution states no law can be enacted that is not compliant with the general principles and objectives of Sharia.<ref>{{cite web|title=UN officials welcome 'historic' approval of new constitution for Somalia|url=https://www.un.org/apps/news/story.asp?NewsID=42603#.VBxVFSiRvP0|publisher=United Nations News Center (2012)|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170704172956/http://www.un.org/apps/news/story.asp?NewsID=42603#.VBxVFSiRvP0|archive-date=2017-07-04|url-status=live|date = August 2012}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|title=Federal Republic of Somalia – Provisional Constitution |url=http://unpos.unmissions.org/LinkClick.aspx?fileticket=RkJTOSpoMME= |publisher=United Nations (2012) |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150402122013/http://unpos.unmissions.org/LinkClick.aspx?fileticket=RkJTOSpoMME%3D |archive-date=2 April 2015 }}</ref> Sharia currently influences all aspects of ''[[Xeer]]'' as well as Somalia's formal legal system.<ref>{{cite book|author1=Abdul Wahid Sh. ===Sudan===
Sharia has been previously declared the chief source of all legislation in Sudan's 1968, 1973 and 1998 Constitutions.<ref>{{cite journal|last1=Clark Lombardi|title=Sharia – A or The Chief Source of Legislation|journal=International Law Review|date=2013|volume=28|issue=3|pages=751–53|url=http://www.auilr.org/pdf/28/28.3.3.pdf|publisher=American University|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140915030843/http://www.auilr.org/pdf/28/28.3.3.pdf|archive-date=2014-09-15|url-status=dead}}</ref> In 2005, [[Sudan]] adopted an interim national constitution; it removed some references to Sharia, but included Sharia-derived criminal, civil and personal legal codes, as well as Sharia-mandated ''hudud'' punishments.<ref>{{cite book|last1=Koendgen|first1=Olaf|editor1-last=Otto|editor1-first=Jan|title=Sharia Incorporated|url=https://archive.org/details/shariaincorporat00otto|url-access=limited|publisher=Leiden University Press|isbn=978-9087280574|pages=[https://archive.org/details/shariaincorporat00otto/page/n181 181]–230|year=2010}}</ref> The [[Criminal Act 1991 (Sudan)|Criminal Act of 1991]] prescribes punishments which include forty lashes for drinking alcohol, amputation of the right hand for theft of a certain value and stoning for adultery.<ref name="Otto2010">{{cite book|author=Jan Michiel Otto|title=Sharia Incorporated: A Comparative Overview of the Legal Systems of Twelve Muslim Countries in Past and Present|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=8ep7cX3ma0sC&pg=PA211|year=2010|publisher=Amsterdam University Press|isbn=978-9087280574|pages=211–212|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160515175729/https://books.google.com/books?id=8ep7cX3ma0sC&pg=PA211|archive-date=15 May 2016|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|title=The Criminal Act 1991|url=http://www.pclrs.org/downloads/bills/Criminal%20Law/Criminal%20Act%201991%20English.pdf|publisher=PCLRS|access-date=21 February 2013|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130526165737/http://www.pclrs.org/downloads/bills/Criminal%20Law/Criminal%20Act%201991%20English.pdf|archive-date=26 May 2013|url-status=dead}}</ref> However, there is no record of either any amputation or stoning ever having taken place in Sudan.<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://mic.com/articles/68431/women-around-the-world-are-being-stoned-to-death-do-you-know-the-facts#.B062VQHfy |title=Women Around the World Are Being Stoned to Death. Do You Know the Facts? |date=16 October 2013 |access-date=20 May 2018 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170727051443/https://mic.com/articles/68431/women-around-the-world-are-being-stoned-to-death-do-you-know-the-facts#.B062VQHfy |archive-date=27 July 2017 |url-status=live }}</ref>

The use of Sharia in Sudan ended in September 2020, when Sudan officially became a secular state after Sudan's transitional government agreed to separate religion from the state, ending 30 years of Islamic rule and Islam as the official state religion in the North African nation.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://gulfnews.com/world/africa/sudan-ends-30-years-of-islamic-law-by-separating-religion-state-1.1599359147751|title=Sudan ends 30 years of Islamic law by separating religion, state|date=6 September 2020 }}</ref><ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.middleeastmonitor.com/20200907-sudan-separates-religion-from-state-ending-30-years-of-islamic-rule/|title=Sudan separates religion from state ending 30 years of Islamic rule/date:7 September 2020|date=7 September 2020 }}</ref><ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.firstpost.com/world/islamic-world-at-decisive-point-in-history-will-it-take-the-path-of-emirates-or-turkey-8789981.html|title=Islamic world at decisive point in history: Will it take the path of Emirates or Turkey?|date=6 September 2020}}</ref> It also scrapped the apostasy law and public flogging.<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.bbc.com/news/world-africa-53379733|title=Sudan scraps apostasy law and alcohol ban for non-Muslims|work=BBC News|date=12 July 2020}}</ref>

However, after 1 year of military coup took place, Sharia got installed once again and harsh punishment like floggings still exist according to article 146.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://morningstarnews.org/2022/05/christian-couple-in-sudan-faces-possible-flogging-for-adultery/|title=Christian Couple in Sudan Faces Possible Flogging for 'Adultery'|date=3 May 2022}}</ref>

==Asia==

===Afghanistan===
{{update|date=August 2021}} 
Under the rule of the [[Taliban]] in Afghanistan, [[Sharia law]] is the sole legal framework governing both criminal and civil matters. The legal system is based entirely on the principles of [[Islamic law]], primarily following the [[Hanafi]] school of jurisprudence. All offenses are adjudicated in accordance with Sharia, with the application of [[hudud]] (fixed punishments), [[qisas]] (retributive justice), and [[tazir]] (discretionary punishment).

In 2024, following Taliban recapture of power in 2021, they issued the Law on the Promotion of Virtue and the Prevention of Vice, comprising 35 articles, which included prohibiting the publication or taking of images of living beings in the media as a violation of Sharia law.<ref>{{Cite news |date=2024-10-14 |title=En Afghanistan, les autorités talibanes promettent d'interdire les images d'êtres vivants dans les médias |url=https://www.lemonde.fr/international/article/2024/10/14/en-afghanistan-les-autorites-talibanes-promettent-d-interdire-les-images-d-etres-vivants-dans-les-medias_6351633_3210.html |access-date=2026-06-15 |language=fr}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |last=admin |date=2025-03-25 |title=UN Report: Promotion of Virtue & The Prevention of Vice |url=https://afghanistanjustice.org/resources/un-report-promotion-of-virtue-the-prevention-of-vice/ |access-date=2026-06-15 |website=Afghanistan Justice Archive |language=en}}</ref>

===Bahrain===
Article 2 of Bahrain's 2002 Constitution as originally adopted, as well as after February 2012 amendment, declares Islamic Sharia is a chief source of legislation.<ref name=clarklombardi/><ref name=bahrainlaw/> Four tiers of ordinary courts have jurisdiction over cases related to civil, administrative and criminal matters, with Court of Cassation the highest civil court in Bahrain; in all matters, the judges are required to resort to Sharia in case legislation is silent or unclear.<ref name=bahrainlaw>{{cite web|title=Chapter III – Relevant Aspects of the Legal System and Description of the Enforcement Structures|url=http://www.govaction.bh/wps/themes/html/EgovBICI/en/pdfs/C3.pdf|publisher=Bahrain Government|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160324203829/https://www.govaction.bh/wps/themes/html/EgovBICI/en/pdfs/C3.pdf|archive-date=2016-03-24|url-status=live}}</ref> Sharia courts handle personal status laws.<ref>{{cite book|author=Gerhard Robbers|title=Encyclopedia of World Constitutions|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=M3A-xgf1yM4C&pg=PA73|access-date=20 August 2013|year=2006|publisher=Infobase Publishing|isbn=978-0816060788|page=73|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160527195011/https://books.google.com/books?id=M3A-xgf1yM4C&pg=PA73&dq|archive-date=27 May 2016|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|title=Bahrain|url=http://www.freedomhouse.org/sites/default/files/inline_images/Bahrain.pdf|publisher=Freedom House|page=4|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181002004344/https://freedomhouse.org/sites/default/files/inline_images/Bahrain.pdf|archive-date=2018-10-02|url-status=live}}</ref>

A personal status law was codified in 2009 to regulate personal status matters. It applies only to Sunni Muslims; there is no codified personal status law for Shiites. In a Sharia court a Muslim woman's testimony is worth half of that of a Muslim man.<ref>{{cite web|title=Bahrain Gender Equality Profile|url=http://www.unicef.org/gender/files/Bahrain-Gender-Eqaulity-Profile-2011.pdf|publisher=UNICEF|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170620145410/https://www.unicef.org/gender/files/Bahrain-Gender-Eqaulity-Profile-2011.pdf|archive-date=2017-06-20|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Bangladesh===
[[Bangladesh]] has a secular constitution but marriage, divorce, alimony and property inheritance are regulated by Sharia for Muslims. The Constitution also declared Islam as its state religion.<ref>{{cite magazine |title=Women and property rights: Who owns Bangladesh? |url=https://www.economist.com/blogs/banyan/2013/08/women-and-property-rights |magazine=The Economist |type=Blog |date=21 August 2013 |access-date=2 December 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131207191111/http://www.economist.com/blogs/banyan/2013/08/women-and-property-rights |archive-date=2013-12-07 |url-status=live }}</ref> The Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937 (XXVI of 1937) applies to Muslims in all matters relating to family affairs.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/bangladesh.htm |title=A Research Guide to the Legal System of the People's Republic of Bangladesh |website=GlobaLex |publisher=New York University School of Law |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130319151229/http://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/Bangladesh.htm |archive-date=19 March 2013 |url-status=dead }}</ref> Islamic family law is applied through the regular court system.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/bangladesh.htm |title=Bangladesh |publisher=Law.emory.edu |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140621041521/http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/bangladesh.htm |archive-date=2014-06-21 |url-status=live }}</ref> There are no limitations on interfaith marriages.<ref>{{cite book|author=Jonathan Fox|title=A World Survey of Religion and the State|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=rE0NcgxNaKEC&pg=PA197|year=2008|publisher=Cambridge University Press|isbn=978-1139472593|page=197|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160603012725/https://books.google.com/books?id=rE0NcgxNaKEC&pg=PA197|archive-date=3 June 2016|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Brunei===
Sharia courts decide personal status cases or cases relating to religious offences.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/brunei.htm |title=Brunei |publisher=Law.emory.edu |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130212080911/http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/brunei.htm |archive-date=2013-02-12 |url-status=live }}</ref> Sultan [[Hassanal Bolkiah]] declared in 2011 his wish to establish Islamic criminal law as soon as possible.<ref>{{cite web|title=Islamic Criminal Law in Brunei: Don't delay|url=http://www.thejakartapost.com/news/2011/10/11/islamic-criminal-law-brunei-dont-delay.html|work=The Jakarta Post|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160305131808/http://www.thejakartapost.com/news/2011/10/11/islamic-criminal-law-brunei-dont-delay.html|archive-date=2016-03-05|url-status=live}}</ref> A new penal code enacted in May 2014 prescribes Sharia punishments, including the severing of limbs for property crimes and death by stoning for adultery and homosexuality.<ref name=latimesbrunei>{{cite web|title=Sultan of Brunei imposes harsh Islamic criminal code|url=http://www.latimes.com/world/asia/la-fg-wn-brunei-sharia-law-20140501-story.html|work=Los Angeles Times|access-date=7 May 2014|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140506174624/http://www.latimes.com/world/asia/la-fg-wn-brunei-sharia-law-20140501-story.html|archive-date=2014-05-06|url-status=live|date=May 2014}}</ref>

===India===
[[India]] has a secular constitution but marriage, divorce, alimony and property inheritance are regulated by Sharia for [[Muslim personal law|Muslims]]. ===Indonesia===
[[Aceh]] is the only part of Indonesia to apply Sharia to criminal law. Islamic courts in Aceh had long handled cases of marriage, divorce and inheritance. After special autonomy legislation was passed in 2001, the reach of courts extend to criminal justice.<ref>{{Cite web |url=http://www.crisisgroup.org/~/media/Files/asia/south-east-asia/indonesia/117_islamic_law_and_criminal_justice_in_aceh.ashx |title=Archived copy |access-date=2018-11-13 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160304051340/http://www.crisisgroup.org/~/media/Files/asia/south-east-asia/indonesia/117_islamic_law_and_criminal_justice_in_aceh.ashx |archive-date=2016-03-04 |url-status=live }}</ref> Offences such as being alone with an unrelated member of the opposite gender and gambling can be punished with canings. In 2014, the provincial government of [[Aceh]] extended Sharia's reach to non-Muslims whose offenses also involve a Muslim, which they can choose to be punished voluntarily either by national law or regional-based Islamic law. If a non-Muslim commits an offense not covered by the Indonesian national criminal code, the non-Muslim may choose to be punished by their own customary or religious law or under Aceh provincial Sharia Law.<ref>[http://www.thejakartapost.com/news/2014/02/07/aceh-fully-enforces-sharia.html Aceh fully enforces sharia] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180307183758/http://www.thejakartapost.com/news/2014/02/07/aceh-fully-enforces-sharia.html |date=2018-03-07 }} The Jakarta Post (7 February 2014)</ref><ref>[https://www.economist.com/news/asia/21596577-politics-much-faith-behind-harder-line-laying-down-gods-law Laying down God's law] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170924183938/https://www.economist.com/news/asia/21596577-politics-much-faith-behind-harder-line-laying-down-gods-law |date=2017-09-24 }} The Economist (15 February 2014).See [[Islamic criminal law in Aceh]]</ref> However, since 2022, this practice is de facto removed and caning in Aceh applies for Indonesian Muslims only.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://aceh.antaranews.com/berita/368387/syariat-islam-aceh-di-mata-kontingen-pon-xxi-jauh-dari-stigma-menyeramkan|title=Syariat Islam Aceh di mata kontingen PON XXI: jauh dari stigma menyeramkan|publisher=Antara Aceh|date=2024-09-21|language=id}}</ref>

In other parts of Indonesia, religious courts have jurisdiction over civil cases between Muslim spouses on matters concerning marriage, divorce, reconciliation, and alimony. The competence of religious courts is not exclusive, and parties can apply to District Courts for adjudication on basis of [[Roman Dutch law]] or local [[adat]].<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/indonesia.htm |title=Indonesia |publisher=Law.emory.edu |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130212080900/http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/indonesia.htm |archive-date=2013-02-12 |url-status=live }}</ref> [[Suharto]]'s [[New Order (Indonesia)|New Order]] expanded the reach of Sharia, first with the 1974 Marriage Act, which assigned jurisdiction over the marriage and divorce of Muslims to the Islamic courts ({{langx|id|peradilan agama}}), and with the 1989 Religious Judicature Act, which elevated Islamic courts by making them a parallel legal system, equal to state courts and gave them jurisdiction over inheritance (''wasiyyah''), gifts (''hibah'') and religious endowments.<ref name=mec/> Muslim litigants could originally choose whether to have inheritance questions decided by the Islamic courts or by the civil courts, but a 2006 amendment eliminated this possibility; the same amendment gave Islamic courts new jurisdiction over property disputes, including financial and economic matters.<ref name=mec/> Muslims seeking a divorce must also file their claim in Islamic courts.<ref name=mec/> The Compilation of Islamic Law 1991 ({{langx|id|Kompilasi Hukum Islam}}) regulates marriage, inheritance, and charitable trusts ([[wakaf]]).<ref name=mec/> Sharia falls outside the jurisdiction of the [[Constitutional Court of Indonesia|Constitutional Court]].<ref name=mec>{{cite web|author1=Mark E. Cammack|author2=R. ===Iran===
Article 167 of the [[Constitution of the Islamic Republic of Iran|constitution]] states that all judicial rulings must be based upon "authoritative Islamic sources and authentic fatwa" by the [[Twelver]] [[Ja'afari]] school of jurisprudence.<ref>{{cite book|author=Jonathan Fox|title=A World Survey of Religion and the State|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=rE0NcgxNaKEC&pg=PA231|year=2008|publisher=Cambridge University Press|isbn=978-1139472593|page=231|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160609184958/https://books.google.com/books?id=rE0NcgxNaKEC&pg=PA231|archive-date=9 June 2016|url-status=live}}</ref> Book 2 of the [[Islamic Penal Code of Iran]] is entirely devoted to [[hudud]] punishments.<ref>{{Cite web |url=http://rc.majlis.ir/fa/law/show/845048 |title=قانون مجازات اسلامی |access-date=2018-11-13 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180815105408/http://rc.majlis.ir/fa/law/show/845048 |archive-date=2018-08-15 |url-status=live }}</ref> Iranian application of Sharia has been seen by scholars as highly flexible and directly contradicting traditional interpretations of the Sharia.<ref>{{cite book|last=Abrahamian|first=Ervand|title=A History of Modern Iran|url=https://archive.org/details/historymodernira00abra|url-access=limited|publisher=[[Cambridge University Press]]|location=Cambridge, UK; New York|year=2008|isbn=978-0521528917|page=[https://archive.org/details/historymodernira00abra/page/n221 190]|quote=They allowed women to study abroad on state scholarships. They even passed bills directly contradicting traditional interpretations of the sharia. They eliminated all distinctions between men and women, between Muslims and non-Muslims, in accepting witnesses in court and awarding monetary compensations for damages. They increased the marriageable age for girls to fifteen (from thirteen). They reopened the judiciary to women. They gave them equal rights in divorce courts and permitted them to have custody rights over children under the age of seven. Never before in the Middle East had a freely elected parliament so blatantly challenged basic tenets of the sharia. What is more, they ratified the UN Convention on Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women – the USA has still refused to ratify this highly egalitarian convention. The liberal cause was further bolstered when Ayatollah Youssef Sanai, one of Khomeini’s favorite disciples, came out in full support of women’s rights. He ruled that the law should not differentiate between the sexes, and that women should have the right to become presidents, chief judges, and even Supreme Leaders.}}</ref>

===Iraq===
Article 1 of the Civil Code identifies Sharia as a main source of legislation.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/iraq.htm |title=Iraq, Republic of |publisher=Law.emory.edu |date=16 March 1983 |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130116220220/http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/iraq.htm |archive-date=2013-01-16 |url-status=live }}</ref> The 1958 Code "made polygamy extremely difficult, granted child custody to the mother in case of divorce, [and] prohibited repudiation and marriage under the age of 16."<ref name="unesco1"/> In 1995, Iraq introduced Sharia punishment for certain types of criminal offenses.<ref>{{cite book|author=Jonathan Fox|title=A World Survey of Religion and the State|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=rE0NcgxNaKEC&pg=PA238|year=2008|publisher=Cambridge University Press|isbn=978-1139472593|page=238|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130510035510/http://books.google.com/books?id=rE0NcgxNaKEC&pg=PA238|archive-date=10 May 2013|url-status=live}}</ref>{{clarify|Reason=The cited source makes only a passing mention of this, and it contradicts other sources which do not regard Iraq's criminal law as being sharia-based.|date=April 2019}}

Iraq's legal system is based on Islamic Sunni and Jafari (Shi’ite) interpretations of Sharia.<ref>{{cite web |title=Iraqi Contract and Sharia Law: A blended concept |url=https://ijcjs.com/menu-script/index.php/ijcjs/article/download/479/363/561 |access-date=2024-08-12 |website=ijcjs |publisher=International journal on criminal justice sciences}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.islamopediaonline.org/country-profile/iraq/islam-and-legal-system/religion-law-and-iraq%E2%80%99s-personal-status-code |title=Religion, Law, and Iraq's Personal Status Code |publisher=[[Islamopedia Online]] |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130530225211/http://www.islamopediaonline.org/country-profile/iraq/islam-and-legal-system/religion-law-and-iraq%E2%80%99s-personal-status-code |archive-date=30 May 2013 |url-status=dead }}</ref> Article 41 of the [[Constitution of Iraq|constitution]] allows for personal status matters (such as marriage, divorce and inheritance) to be governed by the rules of each religious group. The article has not yet been put into effect, and a unified personal status law remains in place that builds on the 1959 personal status code.<ref>{{cite web|title=Iraq Gender Equality Profile|url=http://www.unicef.org/gender/files/Iraq-Gender-Eqaulity-Profile-2011.pdf|publisher=UNICEF|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170620180303/https://www.unicef.org/gender/files/Iraq-Gender-Eqaulity-Profile-2011.pdf|archive-date=2017-06-20|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Israel===
Sharia is one of the sources of legislation for Muslim citizens.<ref name="sh">{{cite news|title=Josh Goodman: What Sharia Law in Democracies Tells Us About Islam|url=http://www.huffingtonpost.com/josh-goodman/post_965_b_744095.html|publisher=HuffPost Religion|date=29 September 2010|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160305024952/http://www.huffingtonpost.com/josh-goodman/post_965_b_744095.html|archive-date=2016-03-05|url-status=live}}</ref> Sharia is binding on personal law issues for Muslim citizens.<ref name="sh" /> The Sharia Courts of Israel arose as a continuation of the Ottoman Sharia courts, whose jurisdiction was restricted under the [[Mandate for Palestine|British Mandate]]. The Sharia Courts operate under the jurisdiction of the Ministry of Justice and adjudicate matters relating to marriages, divorce, financial maintenance, legal capacity and guardianship, custody of children, paternity, prevention of domestic violence, conversion to Islam, and inheritance, among others.<ref name="The Sharia Courts" />

===Jordan===
Jordan has [[Sharia]] [[courts]] and civil courts. Sharia courts have jurisdiction over personal status laws, cases concerning [[Diyya|Diya]] (blood money in cases of crime where both parties are Muslims, or one is and both the Muslim and non-Muslim consent to Sharia court's jurisdiction), and matters pertaining to Islamic Waqfs.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.law.yale.edu/rcw/rcw/jurisdictions/asw/jordan/jordan_const_eng.pdf|title=Jordan, Hashemite Kingdom of|publisher=Law.yale.edu|page=21|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150616070132/http://www.law.yale.edu/rcw/rcw/jurisdictions/asw/jordan/jordan_const_eng.pdf|archive-date=2015-06-16|url-status=dead}}</ref> The Family Law in force is the Personal Status Law of 1976, which is based on Sharia.<ref name="unesco1">{{cite web|title=Women in Personal Status Laws: Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon, Palestine, Syria|url=http://www.unesco.org/new/fileadmin/MULTIMEDIA/HQ/SHS/pdf/Women_in_Personal_Status_Laws.pdf|work=SHS Papers in Women’s Studies/ Gender Research, No. 4|publisher=UNESCO|date=July 2005|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171010052503/http://www.unesco.org/new/fileadmin/MULTIMEDIA/HQ/SHS/pdf/Women_in_Personal_Status_Laws.pdf|archive-date=2017-10-10|url-status=live}}</ref> In Sharia courts, the testimony of two women is equal to that of one man.<ref>{{cite web|title=Jordan Gender Equality Profile|url=http://www.unicef.org/gender/files/Jordan-Gender-Eqaulity-Profile-2011.pdf|publisher=UNICEF|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170620190921/https://www.unicef.org/gender/files/Jordan-Gender-Eqaulity-Profile-2011.pdf|archive-date=2017-06-20|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Lebanon===
Lebanon's legal system is based on a combination of [[Civil law (legal system)|Civil Law]], Sharia and [[Ottoman Empire|Ottoman]] laws.<ref>{{cite web|title=The Lebanese Constitution promulgated on May 23, 1926, with its Amendments|url=http://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/details.jsp?id=6675|publisher=[[World Intellectual Property Organization]]|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170924184247/http://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/details.jsp?id=6675|archive-date=2017-09-24|url-status=live}}</ref> There are eighteen official religions in Lebanon, each with its own family law and religious courts. The Law of 16 July 1962 declares that Sharia governs personal status laws of Muslims, with Sunni and Ja'afari Shia jurisdiction of Sharia.<ref name="unesco1" />

Muslims are required to follow Sharia in family, inheritance and religious matters.<ref>{{cite book|author=Jonathan Fox|title=A World Survey of Religion and the State|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=rE0NcgxNaKEC&pg=PA198|year=2008|publisher=Cambridge University Press|isbn=978-1139472593|page=198|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160527122002/https://books.google.com/books?id=rE0NcgxNaKEC&pg=PA198|archive-date=27 May 2016|url-status=live}}</ref> In 1988, the [[Constitution of Malaysia|constitution]] was amended to state that civil courts cannot hear matters that fall within the jurisdiction of Sharia courts.<ref>{{cite news |last=Pak |first=Jennifer |url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/world-radio-and-tv-14630851 |title=Malaysia's parallel judicial systems come up against legal challenges |work=BBC News |date=5 September 2011 |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130202083826/http://www.bbc.co.uk/news/world-radio-and-tv-14630851 |archive-date=2013-02-02 |url-status=live }}</ref>

===Oman===
Islamic Sharia is the basis for legislation in Oman per Article 2 of its Constitution, and promulgated as Sultani Decree 101/1996.<ref>{{cite web|last1=Mechantaf|first1=Khalil|title=The Legal System and Research in the Sultanate of Oman|url=https://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/Oman.htm|website=GlobaLex|publisher=New York University School of Law|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161015012919/http://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/oman.htm|archive-date=2016-10-15|url-status=live}}</ref> The Personal Statute (Family) Law issued by Royal Decree 97/32 codified provisions of Sharia.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.mofa.gov.om/mofanew/index.asp?id=18 |title=Sultanate of Oman |publisher=Ministry of Foreign Affairs |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080505064242/http://www.mofa.gov.om/mofanew/index.asp?id=18 |archive-date=2008-05-05 |url-status=dead }}</ref> Sharia Court Departments within the civil court system are responsible for personal status matters.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.freedomhouse.org/report/freedom-world/2012/oman |title=Oman |publisher=Freedom House |date= 17 January 2012|access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121025073322/http://www.freedomhouse.org/report/freedom-world/2012/oman |archive-date=2012-10-25 |url-status=live }}</ref> A 2008 law stipulates that the testimonies of men and women before a court are equal.<ref>{{cite web|title=Oman Gender Equality Profile|url=http://www.unicef.org/gender/files/Oman-Gender-Eqaulity-Profile-2011.pdf|publisher=UNICEF|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170620174153/https://www.unicef.org/gender/files/Oman-Gender-Eqaulity-Profile-2011.pdf|archive-date=2017-06-20|url-status=live}}</ref>

Oman's criminal law is based on a combination of Sharia and English common law.<ref name="Newman2010">{{cite book|author=Graeme R. Newman|title=Crime and Punishment around the World &#91;4 volumes&#93;: &#91;Four Volumes&#93;|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=2uK6bR9byVIC&pg=PA336|date=19 October 2010|publisher=ABC-CLIO|isbn=978-0313351341|pages=336–37|access-date=12 August 2015|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160617131202/https://books.google.com/books?id=2uK6bR9byVIC&pg=PA336|archive-date=17 June 2016|url-status=live}}</ref> Omani commercial law is largely based on Sharia; Article 5 of its Law of Commerce defaults to primacy of Sharia in cases of confusion, silence or conflict.

===Pakistan===
The [[Constitution of Pakistan]] acknowledges God as the sole sovereign of the universe and the Parliament as a delegate. The Constitution of Pakistan requires that all laws conform with Islam and not conflict with the Quran or Sunnah. The Council of Islamic Ideology reviewed the British era legislation and found most of it did not conflict with Sharia.<ref>Rubya Mehdi, The Islamization of the Law in Pakistan (2013) Routledge, p. 209</ref> Sharia was declared the Supreme Law of Pakistan in the 1991 Enforcement of Shariat Act. Section 4 stipulates that courts select an interpretation of law consistent with Islamic jurisprudence and principles.<ref>Salma Waheedi & Kristen Stilt, Judicial review in the context of constitutional Islam, in "Comparative Judicial Review", Edward Elgar Publishing, pg. 137</ref>

The Federal Shariat Court was formed to judge the [[conformity]] of Pakistani laws with Islam according to the Quran and Sunnah.<ref>Shahbaz Ahmad Cheema, The Federal Shariat Court’s Role to Determine the Scope of ‘Injunctions of Islam’ and Its Implications, Journal of Islamic State Practices in International Law (2013), p. 95</ref> It has appellate, original and revisional jurisdiction. It consists of 8 judges including three ulama who are required to be qualified in Sharia.<ref>Shahbaz Ahmad Cheema, The Federal Shariat Court’s Role to Determine the Scope of ‘Injunctions of Islam’ and Its Implications, Journal of Islamic State Practices in International Law (2013), p. 92</ref> The Federal Shariat Court invalidated 55 federal statutes and 212 provincial statutes over 30 years.<ref name="auto1">Sadaf Aziz, The Constitution of Pakistan: A Contextual Analysis, Bloomsbury Publishing</ref> Originally certain legislative documents such as the Muslim Personal Law, the Constitution, fiscal and procedural laws were excluded from the original jurisdiction of the Federal Shariat Court.<ref>Shahbaz Ahmad Cheema, The Federal Shariat Court’s Role to Determine the Scope of ‘Injunctions of Islam’ and Its Implications, Journal of Islamic State Practices in International Law (2013), p. 94</ref>  In its 1994 judgement the Supreme Court declared that the term "Muslim personal law" used in Article 203B, which excludes the Federal Shariat Court's jurisdiction under Article 203D only referred to the personal law of each Muslim sect according to their interpretation of the Quran and Sunnah. Hence, the Federal Shariat Court judgement in 2000 held that all other legislation which applied to Muslims in general were under the jurisdiction of the Federal Shariat Court under Article 203D. The court also held that the Constitution had not intended to reduce the role of the Federal Shariat Court.<ref>Elisa Giunchi, Adjudicating Family Law in Muslim Courts, Routledge, p. 80</ref>

Pakistani evidence law has been brought into conformity with Sharia. However, only 9 sections needed to be changed from the British era 1872 evidence act. The differences in the Islamic version focus more on rules concerning the number, character and competence of eyewitnesses. For example, in financial or future matters, the Law of Evidence says that evidence must be attested to by two men, or one man and two women. The Law of Evidence also requires a court to determine competence of a witness by reference to the Islamic injunctions in the Quran and Sunnah.<ref>Aarij S. Wasti, The Hudood Laws of Pakistan: A Social and Legal Misfit in Today's Society, Dalhousie Journal of Legal Studies, pp. 76-77</ref>

Qisas and Diyat laws prescribe retaliatory punishments or compensatory blood money for offenses such as murder and injury.<ref name="auto1" /> The Qisas and Diyat laws in Pakistan make offences such as murder a private offence against the victim and their family, instead of classifying them as a public offence against the state as it had been during the British rule who had abandoned the Qisas and Diyat laws because they made enforcing law and order difficult since the victim or victim's family could pardon or take blood money as compensation. The Qisas and Diyat laws were first introduced by presidential ordinance in 1990 and were then enshrined in law in 1997.<ref>Tahir Wasti, The Application of Islamic Criminal Law in Pakistan: Sharia in Practice (2009) BRILL, Chapter 6</ref>

Most offences in Pakistani law fall under tazir and siyasah. The Pakistan Penal Code is regarded as a code of tazir offences although it includes siyasah offences too even though the term siyasah is not mentioned.<ref>Khan, A. U., Ullah, S., & Abdullah, K. (2018). Personal-cum-political security & blasphemy laws in pakistan: A critical analysis. Pakistan Journal of Criminology, 10(1), 121.</ref> The Hudood Ordinance divides the crimes of zina, qazf, drinking and theft into two categories: those liable to hadd and those liable to tazir. Shariah leaves tazir punishments to discretion.<ref>Rubya Mehdi, The Islamization of the Law in Pakistan (2013) Routledge, pp. 109-110</ref> If the strict evidentiary requirements for hadd offences are not fulfilled, the tazir (discretionary) punishments can be awarded to hadd offences too. Tazir punishments can include forms of punishments such as fines, imprisonment and whipping.<ref>Aarij S. Wasti, The Hudood Laws of Pakistan: A Social and Legal Misfit in Today's Society, Dalhousie Journal of Legal Studies, pp. 74-75</ref>

There are very strict conditions for imposing the hadd punishment of amputation on thieves. The principle used is that the accused will be spared from hadd punishment for any minor doubt and the court will use all legitimate means to avoid imposing a hadd punishment. For this reason, no amputation has ever been occurred for theft. Theft which is committed under pressure is not liable to hadd. Other factors taken into consideration are the relationship between a victim and the culprit as well as the value of the stolen property.  However, Section 13 of the Ordinance states that theft which is not liable to hadd can be punished under tazir. The punishment under Pakistan Penal Code such as fines or imprisonment are applied to theft liable to tazir. Pakistani law also classifies robbery (harabah) as a hadd offence.<ref>Rubya Mehdi, The Islamization of the Law in Pakistan (2013) Routledge</ref>

President [[Zia ul Haq]] introduced the Zakat system to Pakistan through the Zakat and Ushr Ordinance (1980). In Ramadan Pakistani banks deduct 2.5 percent from bank accounts above the ''Nisab'' amount. The banks send the collection to the Ministry of Finance. The Zakat and Ushr Ordinance specify which categories of people are eligible for receiving zakat. These include religious students, orphans and the sick. Pakistani ushr law levies 5 percent tax on harvests from artificially irrigated lands and 10 percent tax on lands which are not artificially irrigated.<ref>Anita M. Weiss, Islamic Reassertion in Pakistan: The Application of Islamic Laws in a Modern State (1986) Syracuse University Press, pg. 74</ref>

Pakistan eliminated interest from domestic transactions but not from foreign debt. Profit and loss sharing schemes and contract markups were started. However, the ulama considered profit and loss to also be a form of riba. The Finance Minister Ghulam Ishaq Khan admitted this in 1984 and said that illegal profiteering and hoarding had to be eliminated. In 1991, the Federal Shariat Court declared riba to be haram and struck down 32 financial laws for being unIslamic. Two private banks challenged this ruling on the grounds that while they accepted the ruling, there was no alternative available. The federal law minister stated: "the government does not challenge the contention that interest is against the Quran, but the system is so deeply entrenched that it cannot be eliminated overnight."<ref>Iftikhar Malik, State and Civil Society in Pakistan: Politics of Authority, Ideology and Ethnicity (1996) Springer, p. 53</ref>

Punishment for apostasy have been effectuated through Pakistan's blasphemy laws.<ref>Forte, D. F. (1994), Apostasy and Blasphemy in Pakistan, Conn. Journal of Int'l Law, Vol. 10, p. 28</ref> In 2006 the government sent a bill to the parliamentary committee for consideration which would impose the death penalty on apostates. The bill has not been passed yet.<ref>Clarke, Ben (2009). "Law, Religion and Violence: A Human Rights-Based Response to Punishment (by State and Non-State Actors) of Apostasy". Adelaide Law Review. 30: 140.</ref> The principle is that a lacuna in statute law is to be filled with Sharia. Martin Lau hypothesized that even though there was no statutory provision concerning apostasy, it may already be punishable by death in Pakistan because of this principle.<ref>Martin Lau, The Role of Islam in the Legal System of Pakistan, BRILL, pg. 137</ref> In 2010, the Federal Shariat Court held that apostasy and treason are both hadd offences.<ref>Cheema, Shahbaz Ahmad. "The Federal Shariat Court's Role to Determine the Scope of 'Injunctions of Islam' and Its Implications". Journal of Islamic State Practices in International Law: 100.</ref>

In 2010, the Court also invalidated those sections of the 2006 Women's Protection Act which had overrode the clauses of the zina and qazf Ordinances.<ref>Gianluca P. Parolin, Religion and the Sources of Law: Shariah in Constitutions, in "Law, Religion, Constitution: Freedom of Religion, Equal Treatment, and the Law" (2016) Routledge, p. 103</ref>

===Turkey===
As part of [[Atatürk's reforms]], Sharia was abolished in April 1924, with the ''Law Regarding the Abolition of Islamic Law Courts and Amendments Regarding the Court Organization''.<ref name="Grigoriadis2012">{{cite book|author=Ioannis N. Grigoriadis|title=Instilling Religion in Greek and Turkish Nationalism: A "Sacred Synthesis"|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=IAWLUF8Hsb8C&pg=PA63|year=2012|publisher=Palgrave Macmillan|isbn=978-1137301192|page=63|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160425010303/https://books.google.com/books?id=IAWLUF8Hsb8C&pg=PA63|archive-date=25 April 2016|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Turkmenistan===
Article 11 of the [[Constitution of Turkmenistan|constitution]] declares that religious groups are separate from the state and the state educational system. But the legal system is civil law with Islamic influences.<ref>{{Cite web |url=http://www.nationmaster.com/country-info/stats/Government/Legal-system |title=Archived copy |access-date=2018-11-13 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180405090026/http://www.nationmaster.com/country-info/stats/Government/Legal-system |archive-date=2018-04-05 |url-status=live }}</ref><ref>{{cite book|author=Jonathan Fox|title=A World Survey of Religion and the State|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=rE0NcgxNaKEC&pg=PA276|year=2008|publisher=Cambridge University Press|isbn=978-1139472593|pages=146–48|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160428010356/https://books.google.com/books?id=rE0NcgxNaKEC&pg=PA276|archive-date=28 April 2016|url-status=live}}</ref>

===United Arab Emirates===
{{main|Legal system of the United Arab Emirates}}
{{Primary sources section

Since September 2020, Sharia is applied to matters of personal status for Muslims and to determine blood money amounts, while other aspects of the law, including criminal law and non-Muslim personal status is governed by civil law. The federal and local court systems comprises Sharia courts and civil courts.<ref name=":73">{{Cite web |title=Federal Decree Law No. (15) of 2020 |url=https://www.moj.gov.ae/assets/2021/Federal%20Decree%20Law%20No.%20(15)%20of%202020%20Concerning%20the%20Penal%20Code.pdf.aspx |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230531203636/https://www.moj.gov.ae/assets/2021/Federal%20Decree%20Law%20No.%20(15)%20of%202020%20Concerning%20the%20Penal%20Code.pdf.aspx |archive-date=31 May 2023 |access-date=8 June 2023 |website=Ministry of Justice |at=Pages 1, Article 1 "The provisions of the Islamic Shari’a shall apply to the retribution and blood money crimes. Other crimes and their respective punishments shall be provided for in accordance with the provisions of this Law and other applicable penal codes". |publication-date=27 September 2020}}</ref>

In September 2020, corporal punishment was officially removed as a legal form of punishment under UAE federal law. Article 1 of the Federal Penal Code was amended in 2020 to state that Sharia applies only to retribution and blood money punishments and the decree defined legal forms of punishment are retribution and blood money punishments, capital punishment, life imprisonment, temporary imprisonment incarceration, detention, and fines.<ref name=":732">{{Cite web |title=Federal Decree Law No. (15) of 2020 |url=https://www.moj.gov.ae/assets/2021/Federal%20Decree%20Law%20No.%20(15)%20of%202020%20Concerning%20the%20Penal%20Code.pdf.aspx |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230531203636/https://www.moj.gov.ae/assets/2021/Federal%20Decree%20Law%20No.%20(15)%20of%202020%20Concerning%20the%20Penal%20Code.pdf.aspx |archive-date=31 May 2023 |access-date=8 June 2023 |website=Ministry of Justice |at=Pages 1, Article 1 "The provisions of the Islamic Shari’a shall apply to the retribution and blood money crimes. Other crimes and their respective punishments shall be provided for in accordance with the provisions of this Law and other applicable penal codes". |publication-date=27 September 2020}}</ref> Previously the article stated that "provisions of the Islamic Law shall apply to the crimes of doctrinal punishment, punitive punishment and blood money"<ref name=":732" /><ref>{{cite web |title=Federal Law No (3) of 1987 on Issuance of the Penal Code |url=https://www.unodc.org/tldb/showDocument.do?documentUid=6385&country=UAE |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130525044622/https://www.unodc.org/tldb/showDocument.do?documentUid=6385&country=UAE |archive-date=25 May 2013 |work=United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime}}</ref> making flogging, stoning, amputation, and [[crucifixion]] technically legal punishments for criminal offences such as adultery, [[premarital sex|pre-marital sex]], and drug or alcohol consumption.<ref name="usdep">{{cite web |title=2013 Human Rights Reports: United Arab Emirates |url=https://2009-2017.state.gov/j/drl/rls/hrrpt/2013/nea/220380.htm |work=[[US Department of State]] |quote=Sharia (Islamic law) courts, which adjudicate criminal and family law, have the option of imposing flogging as punishment for adultery, [[prostitution]], consensual premarital sex, pregnancy outside marriage, defamation of character, and drug or alcohol abuse.}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |title=U.N. Commission on Crime Prevention and Criminal Justice: United Arab Emirates |url=http://www.humanrightsvoices.org/EYEontheUN/un_101/figures/?p=2484 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201111230016/http://www.humanrightsvoices.org/EYEontheUN/un_101/figures/?p=2484 |archive-date=11 November 2020 |access-date=12 February 2016 |work=Human Rights Voices}}</ref>

===Uzbekistan===
Uzbekistan has a civil law system.<ref name="wf-legal" />

===Yemen===
Law 20/1992 regulates personal status. The [[Constitution of Yemen|constitution]] mentions Sharia.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.islamopediaonline.org/country-profile/yemen/islam-and-legal-system/islam-and-law-after-unification-debates-and-challenges |title=Islam and Law After Unification: Debates and Challenges |publisher=Islamopedia Online |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150711051922/http://www.islamopediaonline.org/country-profile/yemen/islam-and-legal-system/islam-and-law-after-unification-debates-and-challenges |archive-date=11 July 2015 |url-status=dead }}</ref> Penal law provides for application of hadd penalties for certain crimes, although the extent of implementation is unclear.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/yemen.htm |title=Yemen, Republic of |publisher=Law.emory.edu |access-date=18 February 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130212064852/http://www.law.emory.edu/ifl/legal/yemen.htm |archive-date=2013-02-12 |url-status=live }}</ref> Article 263 of the 1994 penal code states that "the adulterer and adulteress without suspicion or coercion are punished with whipping by one hundred strokes as a penalty if not married. [...] If the adulterer or the adulteress are married, they are punished by stoning them to death."<ref>{{cite web|title=Republican Decree, By Law No. 12 for 1994, Concerning Crimes and Penalties|url=http://www.unhcr.org/refworld/docid/3fec62f17.html|publisher=UNHCR Refworld|access-date=21 February 2013}}</ref>

==Europe==

===Bosnia and Herzegovina===
In spite of the fact that the country has a slight Muslim majority, it has a civil law system.<ref name="wf-legal" />

===Germany===
Under certain conditions, Sharia rules on [[domestic relations]] are recognized by German courts based on [[conflict of marriage laws|private international law]] if no party has German citizenship. As a result, cases such as marriage, divorce, as well as many personal cases cannot be handled under German law if it is between Muslims who are not citizens. The outcome in most circumstances must not violate the principles of the German legal system according to the [[ordre public]].<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.goethe.de/lhr/prj/daz/mag/igd/en9650179.htm |title=Sharia Courts in Western Democracies? – An Interview with Manfred Brocker |date=November 2012 |access-date=2018-11-13 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181004192955/http://www.goethe.de/lhr/prj/daz/mag/igd/en9650179.htm |archive-date=2018-10-04 |url-status=live }}</ref>

===Greece===
[[File:Location map of Thrace (Greece).svg|alt=Map of Western Thrace region in Greece|thumb|316x316px|{{legend|#5f8dd3|Sharia applies in personal status issues (Muslim minority only)}}
{{legend|#e6e6e6|Sharia does not apply (Muslims subject to civil law)}}]]
In [[Western Thrace]], under the terms of the 1920 [[Treaty of Sevres]] and 1923 [[Treaty of Lausanne]], Sharia courts historically had exclusive jurisdiction over the [[Muslim minority of Greece|Greek Muslim minority]] population in issues related to family law.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.lecourrierdiplomatique.eu/2017/02/sharia-law-thrace/ |title=Sharia Law in Thrace |last=Macris |first=P.C. |work=Le Courrier Diplomatique |date=2017-02-18 |access-date=2017-11-30 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171201062025/http://www.lecourrierdiplomatique.eu/2017/02/sharia-law-thrace/ |archive-date=2017-12-01 |url-status=live }}</ref><ref name="stategreece">{{cite web|title=Greece|url=https://2009-2017.state.gov/documents/organization/171697.pdf|publisher=U.S. Department of State|access-date=16 January 2015|pages=2–4}}</ref> 

Since 2018, Muslims in the region have been given the choice of registering a civil marriage and pursuing civil cases in the national court system.<ref>{{cite news|title=Greece Scraps Compulsory Shariah for Muslim Minority|author=Niki Kitsantonis|newspaper=The New York Times|date=10 January 2018|url=https://www.nytimes.com/2018/01/10/world/europe/greece-shariah-law.html|access-date=16 April 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190416212547/https://www.nytimes.com/2018/01/10/world/europe/greece-shariah-law.html|archive-date=16 April 2019|url-status=live}}</ref><ref name="stategreece" /> The Treaty of Lausanne also allows for the establishment of [[waqf]]s.<ref name="stategreece" />

Greece is the only country in the EU that officially recognizes sharia and the mufti institution.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Papadopoulos |first=Kosta |date=2026-04-10 |title=Greece Is The Only European Country Recognising Mufti Institution Greek City Times |url=https://greekcitytimes.com/2026/04/11/greece-is-the-only-european-country-recognising-mufti-institution/ |access-date=2026-07-21 |website=Greek City Times |language=en-GB}}</ref>

In other parts of Greece, all people are subjected exclusively to the provisions of the civil code, regardless of their religion.<ref name="acadgreece">{{cite web|last1=Kakoulidou|first1=Eirini|title=The application of Shari'ah in the Greek area of Western Thrace|url=https://www.academia.edu/3287627|publisher=Academia.edu|access-date=16 January 2015|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150428075238/http://www.academia.edu/3287627/The_application_of_Shari_ah_in_the_Greek_area_of_Western_Thrace_Protecting_the_religious_freedom_of_the_Muslim_minority_or_dismantling_the_Greek_Constitution|archive-date=28 April 2015|url-status=dead}}</ref>

===United Kingdom===
[[England and Wales]]: Sharia councils, which have no legal status and no legal jurisdiction, are consulted by many Muslims as a source of religious guidance and as an instance granting religious divorces.<ref name="uk-review">{{cite web|website=UK Government|url=https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/678478/6.4152_HO_CPFG_Report_into_Sharia_Law_in_the_UK_WEB.pdf|title=The independent review into the application of sharia law in England and Wales|year=2018|page=4|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181002202500/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/678478/6.4152_HO_CPFG_Report_into_Sharia_Law_in_the_UK_WEB.pdf|archive-date=2018-10-02|url-status=live}}</ref>

Providers of Islamic arbitration services (such as the [[Muslim Arbitration Tribunal]]) must follow existing arbitration legislation (specifically the [[Arbitration Act 1996]]) in order for their decisions to be legally enforceable. The same rule applies to non-religious arbitration providers and other faith-based bodies (including the Jewish ''[[beth din]]'' and Catholic canon law tribunals).<ref>{{cite news |last=Rozenberg |first=Joshua |url=https://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/newstopics/lawreports/joshuarozenberg/2957692/What-can-sharia-courts-do-in-Britain.html |title=What can sharia courts do in Britain? |publisher=Telegraph |date=2008-09-14 |accessdate=2010-01-04 |location=London |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090427073058/http://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/newstopics/lawreports/joshuarozenberg/2957692/What-can-sharia-courts-do-in-Britain.html |archive-date=2009-04-27 |url-status=live }}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |last=Owolade |first=Femi |date=2025-10-30 |title=Sharia law isn't taking over Britain – it's an inevitable legacy of its colonial legal history |url=http://theconversation.com/sharia-law-isnt-taking-over-britain-its-an-inevitable-legacy-of-its-colonial-legal-history-267262 |access-date=2026-02-06 |website=The Conversation |language=en-US}}</ref>

The operation of sharia councils in the UK has been politically controversial. A bill was introduced in the House of Lords by [[Caroline Cox, Baroness Cox]] seeking to restrict religious arbitration and mediation services and make it a criminal offence to falsely claim legal jurisdictions of a court, though it did not progress beyond first reading.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Arbitration and Mediation Services (Equality) Bill [HL] - Parliamentary Bills - UK Parliament |url=https://bills.parliament.uk/bills/1399 |access-date=2026-02-06 |website=bills.parliament.uk |language=en}}</ref> In 2017, the [[UK Independence Party]] stated they would ban sharia courts.<ref>{{Cite news |last=Elgot |first=Jessica |date=2017-04-23 |title=Ukip to campaign to ban burqa and sharia courts, says Paul Nuttall |url=https://www.theguardian.com/politics/2017/apr/23/ukip-to-campaign-to-ban-burka-and-sharia-courts-says-paul-nuttall |access-date=2026-02-06 |work=The Guardian |language=en-GB |issn=0261-3077}}</ref> In 2018, the UK government commissioned a review into the operation of sharia law in England and Wales,<ref name="uk-review" /> and the [[Home Affairs Select Committee]] also conducted a similar inquiry.<ref>{{Cite news |last=Bowcott |first=Owen |date=2016-06-27 |title=MPs launch inquiry into sharia courts in UK |url=https://www.theguardian.com/law/2016/jun/27/mps-launch-inquiry-sharia-law-courts-uk |access-date=2026-02-06 |work=The Guardian |language=en-GB |issn=0261-3077}}</ref>

In 2018, the High Court recognized sharia in a divorce ruling in 2018, noting that a Muslim couple married under Sharia will also be recognized under British law and that the woman may claim her share of assets in a divorce.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/2018/08/01/british-court-recognises-sharia-law-landmark-divorce-case/|title=British court recognises sharia law in landmark divorce case|last=McCann|first=Kate|date=2018-08-01|work=The Telegraph|access-date=2019-01-02|language=en-GB|issn=0307-1235|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190103004839/https://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/2018/08/01/british-court-recognises-sharia-law-landmark-divorce-case/|archive-date=3 January 2019|url-status=live}}</ref> This decision was overturned on appeal.<ref>{{Cite BAILII |litigants=HM Attorney General v Akhter & Ors |court=EWCA |division=Civ |year=2020 |num=122 |date=14 February 2020 |parallelcite=[2021] Fam 277, [2020] 2 WLR 1183, [2020] 2 FLR 139, [2020] 2 FCR 46, [2020] WLR(D) 95 }}</ref>

===Slovenia===

Sharia law is not valid in any circumstance. Slovenia has [https://www.gov.si/en/policies/rule-of-law-and-justice/legal-order/ civil law system].

==Territories with limited recognition==
===Kosovo===
Kosovo uses civil law.<ref name=wf-legal/>

===Northern Cyprus===
It has a secular legal system with heavy influence from the modern-day Turkish legal system. It is unclear if Northern Cyprus is a civil law system, or if it uses common law.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.cypnet.co.uk/ncyprus/main/polsyst/constitution/|title=The Constitution of the Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus|work=cypnet.co.uk|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110525163715/http://www.cypnet.co.uk/ncyprus/main/polsyst/constitution/|archive-date=2011-05-25|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Palestine===
The Egyptian personal status law of 1954 is applied. The personal status law is based on Sharia and regulates matters related to inheritance, marriage, divorce and child custody. Shari’a courts hear cases related to personal status. The testimony of a woman is worth only half of that of a man in cases related to marriage, divorce and child custody.<ref name="opt">{{cite web|title=Occupied Palestinian Territory Gender Equality Profile|url=http://www.unicef.org/gender/files/oPT-Gender-Eqaulity-Profile-2011.pdf|publisher=UNICEF|access-date=2018-11-13|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170620154451/https://www.unicef.org/gender/files/oPT-Gender-Eqaulity-Profile-2011.pdf|archive-date=2017-06-20|url-status=live}}</ref>

===[[Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic]]===
Local [[qadi]]s ([[Sharia]] judges) have jurisdiction over personal status and family law issues.<ref name="hrw_2008">{{Cite web|title=Human Rights in Western Sahara and in the Tindouf Refugee Camps|url=https://www.hrw.org/report/2008/12/19/human-rights-western-sahara-and-tindouf-refugee-camps|date=19 December 2008|website=Human Rights Watch|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150613030817/http://www.hrw.org/en/reports/2008/12/19/human-rights-western-sahara-and-tindouf-refugee-camps-0|archive-date=13 June 2015|publisher=Human Right Watch|access-date=13 August 2024|url-status=live}}</ref>
